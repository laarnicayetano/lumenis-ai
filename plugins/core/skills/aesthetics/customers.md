# Aesthetics — Customers

Who Aesthetics actually sells to and serves. Practice/channel types and most patient framing are sourced from lumenis.com (resource hub, news, and product pages); the patient-segment data below (menopause, GLP-1, male patient, hair loss, regenerative/wellness) is sourced from a third-party syndicated consumer survey, not lumenis.com — see the note at the top of **Patient segments**. This is about _market segments and channels_ — the different kinds of businesses and patient populations involved — not tone. For how to write to a given persona (KOL, Physician, Practice Owner, Patient), see [audience-archetypes/aesthetics-archetypes.md](../audience-archetypes/aesthetics-archetypes.md); this file is the "who," that file is the "how to talk to them."

## Practice / channel types (the buyer)

- **Dermatology practices** — both medical and cosmetic dermatologists. Lumenis frames this as a convergence story: "traditional dermatologists are now embracing aesthetic medicine," i.e. a customer base that didn't used to buy energy-based aesthetic devices increasingly does.
- **Plastic surgery practices** — implied throughout the portfolio (blepharoplasty, scar revision, body contouring) though less explicitly documented as its own segment than dermatology; treat as adjacent to dermatology rather than fully distinct in current materials.
- **Medspas / aesthetic clinics** — spans boutique single-location spas (e.g. "Pura Vida Body Mind Spa," "Skin by Sarah Hudson") up to large multi-location chains. **Australian Skin Clinics** (50 locations, 22+ years in business) is the clearest documented example of the chain end of this segment — they evaluated Lumenis on clinical performance (SPLENDOR X's Blend X technology, 250W output, Dual Cooling System for pain-free treatment) and versatility across skin tones, not on price alone, and cited "strictest training and protocols" as part of their own value proposition to their patients.
- **Luxury / wellness clinics** — a distinct, higher-end tier: the Harrods Wellness Clinic (London) partnership is framed as "a partnership of excellence," positioning Lumenis alongside a premium retail/wellness brand rather than a clinical-only context.
- **Hospital / reconstructive & scar centers** — a smaller but real segment oriented around medical necessity rather than elective cosmetic work (e.g. coverage of the Queen Elizabeth Hospital Birmingham scar center). Sits closer to medical device use than elective aesthetics.

## Patient segments (the end user)

_Sourcing note: the percentages below come from a consumer-research report (NewBeauty/BeautyEngine, "The State of Aesthetics," Summer '26 edition — Q2 2026 survey, sample sizes vary by question, roughly n=230-770). Re-verify against the source report before quoting a specific figure externally._

Cross-cutting context before the segments: today's patient is more informed and self-directed than before — nearly a third have already used AI to research treatments, and practice websites rank just behind search engines as a research source. Peak aesthetic urgency concentrates in the 40s (34.1%) and 50s (28.8%); the large majority (93%) are open to some form of treatment, but most want it subtle and natural-looking (50.6%) rather than a dramatic transformation, and "looking rested" is a near-universal priority (91% call it important). Only 9.1% follow a formal long-term treatment plan with a provider — read that as an unmet-planning gap, not low interest, since word-of-mouth is already doing real work (nearly three-quarters would recommend a treatment to friends/family).

- **General cosmetic/aesthetic patients** — the default assumed audience: adults seeking non-invasive treatment for aging, skin quality, hair removal, body contouring.
- **Menopause patients** — an explicitly named, high-interest segment: energy treatments (lasers, RF microneedling) lead treatment interest at 76.6%, ahead of menopause-specific topical skincare (70.8%), supplements, fillers, and HRT. When evaluating a treatment, efficacy (24.9%) outweighs cost (22.2%) and safety (20.9%) as the top concern — a patient group ready to engage directly once a provider raises the topic in intake.
- **GLP-1 / post-weight-loss patients** — a fast-emerging segment tied to weight-loss medication use: 48% of GLP-1 users surveyed have lost 20+ lbs, and loose/sagging skin (42%) and muscle tone/definition (40.4%) are their top post-weight-loss body concerns — skin tightening is their leading aesthetic interest after weight loss (64%). The broader body ideal is shifting toward lean/athletic (67% combined, vs. 21% curvy), a shift respondents attribute partly to the GLP-1 era.
- **Male patients** — hair restoration is the clearest entry point (72.3% interest among the men respondents described), opening into skin rejuvenation (41.1%), neurotoxin injections (35.4%), jawline/facial contouring (28.4%), and body contouring (25.9%); hair transplant is the single procedure men would most likely consider (42.2%), well ahead of blepharoplasty (20.3%). Note: this is respondent-reported data about "the men in their lives," not a direct male-patient self-report survey — treat it as directional, not a precise male-patient profile.
- **Scar patients** — spans cosmetic (acne scarring) and medical/reconstructive (surgical scars, burn scars) — UltraPulse's SCAAR FX mode and the scar-treatment conference sponsorships (SCARS II, Israel) both point here.
- **Veterans ("wounded warriors")** — a named, specific population: combination therapy (laser hair removal + neuromodulators) framed around quality-of-life improvement, not purely cosmetic outcome. A narrow but distinct segment worth naming precisely rather than folding into "scar patients" generally.
- **Pigmentation/melasma patients** — includes a pregnancy-related angle ("melasma: what you don't expect when you're expecting").
- **Hair loss patients** — the newest patient segment via FoLix, reached partly through the Bosley partnership's existing consumer channel rather than only through practices. Demand is real (65.8% prioritize visible regrowth above every other factor, nearly 4x the next-ranked concern, safety) but underserved clinically: most patients are still starting with the least-invasive options — supplements (71.7%), scalp health treatments (49.5%), OTC treatments (48.1%) — before considering anything procedural, and only 4.7% have tried or are considering surgery. That's an education/conversion gap a clinical, FDA-cleared option is positioned to close, not a sign of weak demand.
- **Regenerative / wellness-curious patients** — a growing crossover segment between aesthetics and longevity/wellness: over half have tried red light therapy in the past year, and peptide therapies top what patients want to learn more about (57.4%), ahead of NAD+ therapies and hormone optimization. Most are still unfamiliar with the specifics of regenerative treatments generally (only 37% call themselves somewhat/very familiar) — an awareness gap rather than a disinterest one, and a real opening for provider education.
- **Implant-revision patients** _(general plastic-surgery-adjacent patient psychology, not tied to a current Lumenis product)_ — worth knowing even without a direct product fit today: nearly three-quarters of existing breast-implant patients have considered revision or replacement, and it's overwhelmingly an aesthetic decision (implant age, appearance changes, size/shape preference) rather than a safety concern (16%) — useful context for how this adjacent patient population thinks about "revision," generally.

## Professional / influence segment

- **KOLs and presenting physicians** — practitioners who adopt early and speak publicly about it: named individual physicians recur throughout the resource hub as case studies and testimonials (e.g. Dr. James Chelnis on triLift, Dr. Elizabeth Grieshaber on Legend Pro ROI, Dr. Duplechain and Dr. Gilbert on UltraPulse). These conference-circuit relationships (AAD, ASDS, ASLMS, WCD) are a distinct channel from the direct-buyer practice — they drive peer credibility and awareness, not just individual sales.
